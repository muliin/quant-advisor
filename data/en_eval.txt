The history of artificial intelligence is usually dated from a summer workshop held at Dartmouth College in nineteen fifty six, although the ideas that converged there had been circulating for more than a decade. Norbert Wiener had already published his work on cybernetics, arguing that feedback and control were the common substrate of both machines and living organisms. Alan Turing had proposed a thought experiment in which a person converses with an unseen interlocutor and must decide whether the replies come from a human or a machine. Neither of these contributions produced a working system, but together they established a vocabulary that the field would use for the next seventy years.

The first two decades were characterized by optimism that now looks naive. Researchers believed that the central difficulty had been identified, and that the remaining work was a matter of engineering. Programs were written that could prove theorems from a small set of axioms, play checkers at a respectable level, and manipulate blocks on a tabletop. Each success was reported as evidence that general intelligence was close at hand. The gap between these demonstrations and ordinary human competence was consistently underestimated, largely because the demonstrations took place in carefully bounded worlds where every object and every legal move had been anticipated by the programmer.

The collapse came when the funding agencies asked for results outside those bounded worlds. A machine translation system that performed acceptably on chemistry papers failed completely on political speeches. A program that planned sequences of actions in a toy domain could not be scaled to a realistic logistics problem without exhausting both memory and time. The critique that eventually became official policy was that the field had confused the tractable with the typical, solving problems that had been chosen because they were solvable rather than because they mattered.

A second period of enthusiasm arrived with expert systems. The insight behind them was that a great deal of professional competence consists of rules of thumb accumulated through experience, and that these rules could be elicited from practitioners and encoded as conditional statements. Systems were built for diagnosing infections, configuring computer hardware, and interpreting mineral assays. For a few years the approach appeared to deliver on its promises, and corporations invested heavily in what were described as knowledge engineering projects. The difficulty was maintenance. A knowledge base with a few hundred rules could be audited by a single expert. One with several thousand rules could not, because the rules interacted in ways that no individual understood, and each new rule risked contradicting an older one in a situation nobody had foreseen.

Machine learning offered a different answer to the same problem. Instead of asking an expert to articulate what they knew, one could present examples and let a procedure infer a rule from them. This inverted the economics of knowledge acquisition. Collecting examples was often cheaper than interviewing experts, and the resulting models degraded gracefully when they encountered unfamiliar inputs, producing a plausible answer rather than a confident contradiction. The limitation was that the inference procedures of that era could only discover relatively simple regularities, and the features they operated on still had to be designed by hand.

Three developments changed that. The first was the realization that a network with many layers could represent a hierarchy of features, with early layers capturing local patterns and later layers composing them into objects and concepts. The second was the discovery that such networks could be trained effectively by propagating error gradients backwards through the layers, provided that the activation functions were chosen carefully and the weights were initialized sensibly. The third was the availability of hardware originally designed for rendering images, which turned out to be far better suited to the matrix arithmetic that these networks required than general purpose processors were.

The consequence was a rapid succession of results that moved the field from the periphery to the center of the technology industry. Within a decade, systems trained on large collections of labelled examples outperformed human specialists at recognizing objects in photographs, transcribing speech, and translating between languages. Each of these tasks had been considered a benchmark of machine intelligence for decades, and each fell in a surprisingly short time once the ingredients were in place.

Language modelling followed a different trajectory. Early systems relied on counting how often sequences of words appeared in a corpus and estimating probabilities from those counts. This worked well for short sequences and poorly for long ones, because the number of possible sequences grows exponentially with their length, and most of them never appear in any corpus however large. The remedy was to represent words as dense vectors and to learn a function that mapped from context to next word, so that similarity between contexts could be exploited rather than requiring exact matches.

The attention mechanism, introduced to improve translation, proved to be the key architectural idea. Its function is to let each position in a sequence gather information from every other position, with the amount gathered determined by a learned comparison between the two. This has two advantages over sequential processing. It removes the requirement that information travel one step at a time, so the distance between any two positions is a single operation regardless of how far apart they are. And it allows the computation at all positions to be performed simultaneously, which is what makes training on modern hardware practical.

Scaling these architectures produced improvements that were remarkably predictable. Performance on held out text improved smoothly as the number of parameters, the amount of training data, and the amount of computation were increased together, with the improvement following a power law over several orders of magnitude. This regularity was useful in practice because it allowed the returns from a proposed training run to be forecast before the run was paid for. It also raised a question that the field is still working on, namely whether the same regularity holds for capabilities that are qualitatively new rather than incrementally better.

Deployment introduced constraints that research had not had to consider. A model with a hundred billion parameters requires two hundred gigabytes of memory simply to store in half precision, which exceeds the capacity of any single accelerator card. Generating text requires additional memory for the intermediate state associated with each position already processed, and that state grows linearly with the length of the conversation. The result is that the cost of serving a model is frequently dominated not by the arithmetic it performs but by the data it must move from memory into the processor, a regime in which faster arithmetic brings no benefit at all.

Quantization addresses this by representing the weights with fewer bits. The immediate saving is in storage, but the more important effect in the deployment regime is that each step of generation reads fewer bytes, so a workload that was limited by memory bandwidth can complete more quickly. The difficulty is that a weight matrix whose entries span a wide range cannot be represented accurately by a small number of levels, and the standard remedy is to divide the matrix into groups and give each group its own scale, at the cost of some additional metadata.

A related question concerns which parts of a network deserve the most precision. Measurements consistently show that some layers can be reduced to four bits with almost no change in output quality, while others degrade sharply when moved from eight bits to four. If the pattern of sensitivity were known in advance, a fixed budget could be allocated unevenly, giving more bits to the layers that need them and fewer to those that do not. Doing this well requires measuring sensitivity layer by layer, which is expensive but far cheaper than evaluating every possible combination of layer bit widths.
